When I ask someone for advice, I ask them to rank. Which of these would you choose, in what order, and why. People sometimes find the request blunt, because the polite habit is to lay out the options with their advantages and drawbacks and let the other person decide. I think that habit is less helpful than it looks.

A list of options with pros and cons gives me back the problem I arrived with. I already knew there were several choices and that each had merits. What I lacked was a judgement about which merits matter most in my situation, and that is precisely the part a balanced list leaves out. The author has done the research and stopped just before the difficult step.

A ranking forces that step. To put one option first, you have to choose a criterion: cost, speed, risk, quality. You have to say that in this case it outweighs the others. That reasoning is where the value is, and it is also where the adviser becomes accountable, which I suspect is why many people avoid it.

I learned to appreciate this in investment work. A memo that describes a company in forty pages and ends without a recommendation is of very little use to the person who has to decide. The useful ones end with a position: do it, at this price, for these reasons, and here is what would change my mind. THE ENTITY works the same way. It gives every company a score and a call instead of a paragraph that could support either conclusion.

I hold myself to the same rule. For any decision that matters, I write three things: the ranking, the criterion that produced it, and the fact that would reverse it. The last one is important. It turns a decision from a mood into something I can revisit honestly when the situation changes.

Ranked answers are also more useful when they are wrong. If I chose the cheapest supplier and it failed, I can see that I weighted price too heavily and adjust. If I had kept all options open and drifted into one, there would be nothing to learn from, because no reasoning was ever written down.

Of course a ranking can be premature. Sometimes I do not know enough, and the right answer is to say what I need to find out first. But that is also a decision, with a next step attached. What I try never to produce, for myself or for anyone else, is a careful description of the choices that leaves the choosing undone.
