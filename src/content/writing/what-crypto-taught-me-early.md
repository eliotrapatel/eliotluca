I came to crypto in 2017, as a teenager, years before I studied finance. Looking back, it was a rough education and a very efficient one. Four lessons from that period still shape how I work.

The first was about custody. In crypto you can hold your own assets, with a key that only you have. Nobody can freeze them, and nobody can restore them if you lose the key or send funds to the wrong address. I learned very young what it means for a mistake to be final and to be mine alone. Most people meet that idea much later, if at all, because banks and institutions absorb their errors for them. I think meeting it early made me more careful and, oddly, more calm. When you know that nobody will fix it, you check before you act.

The second was about emotion. Crypto markets never close, and they can move twenty percent in a day. Watching that happen at an age when any sum felt enormous taught me that my first reaction to a price is almost never worth acting on. I learned to decide in advance how much I was prepared to lose, and to size what I held so that I could sleep. Later, in finance, I heard this called risk management. I had already learned it the unpleasant way.

The third was about incentives. Every crypto project is an experiment in paying people to behave in a certain way: to hold, to lend, to vote, to secure a network. Reading how those systems were designed, and watching them succeed or collapse, showed me very directly that people do what they are rewarded for and that a badly designed reward destroys a good idea. I have looked at companies through that lens ever since.

The fourth was about what is real. In 2017 there were thousands of projects, and most of them were a website and a promise. Sorting through them, I got into the habit of asking what actually exists behind a claim: who uses it, what it does today, what would be left if the price went to zero. That question is the origin of how I think now. It is why I am drawn to businesses with something physical underneath them.

Crypto later became the subject of my thesis, and the mechanics I learned then are in the [Pool](/instruments/pool) instrument on this site. But the most valuable part was never technical. It was learning, early and with real consequences, that money is a matter of responsibility before it is a matter of return.
