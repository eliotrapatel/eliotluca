I speak or have worked in five languages: English, French, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese. People tend to treat this as a practical advantage, useful for travel and for meetings. It is, but the larger benefit is elsewhere, and it has shaped how I build.

The first thing several languages teach you is that an idea is separate from its wording. When you have to say the same thing in French and then in English, you notice that some sentences only worked because of how they sounded in one of them. What survives the translation is the actual idea. I use this deliberately. If I cannot restate a plan in another language without it falling apart, the plan was mostly phrasing.

The second is attention to the other person. When you work in someone's language, even imperfectly, the conversation changes. They relax, they say more, and they explain things they would have skipped in a language that costs them effort. In a negotiation or a supplier meeting, that difference decides how much you really learn. It also teaches a kind of humility that is useful in business: for a while you are the slower person in the room, and you find out how much people will help someone who is making an effort.

The third is the one that affects my companies most. Both of them are meant for the whole world from the start. ORBES is sold online worldwide. THE ENTITY is written for investors anywhere. When you know your words will be read by people for whom English is a second or third language, you write differently. You choose short sentences and common words, and you avoid jokes and references that only work in one culture. This is the reason ORBES speaks in so few words, and the reason THE ENTITY explains a complicated subject in plain English. Simplicity, for me, started as a courtesy to readers abroad, and it turned out to make the writing better for everyone.

Learning languages also showed me how to learn anything else. The method is always the same: get the fundamentals properly, then use them every day in real situations, badly at first. Nobody becomes fluent by studying grammar alone, and I have found that nobody becomes competent in design or finance by reading about them either. I approached each of my disciplines the way I would approach a new language.

I expect to add more. Each language gives access to people and to ways of thinking that stay closed otherwise, and that is worth far more than the convenience it is usually praised for.
