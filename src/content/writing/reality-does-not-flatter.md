I say that I build on what is tangible. It could pass for a preference, the way some people like wood and others like glass. It is a conviction about where truth is found.

Most of what surrounds us can be argued with. An opinion can be defended. A forecast can be revised. A story about a company can be told so well that it raises money for years before anyone checks it. Much of the modern economy runs on narratives, and narratives are answerable only to other narratives.

A physical thing is different, because it answers back. A bridge carries the load or it does not. A ring fits the finger or it does not. A factory shipped the parts this month or it did not. There is nobody to persuade. I can be as eloquent as I like about a watch movement, and it will still stop if one wheel is wrong. What I value in matter is that it is indifferent to my explanations.

This is why I trust the physical side of things as the place where claims are finally settled. Behind every story about artificial intelligence there is a building that needs electricity, and the electricity is there or it is not. Behind every brand there is an object someone will hold, and the object is well made or it is not. When I want to know what is true about a business, I go looking for the part of it that cannot be talked into existence.

There is a moral side to this as well. Working with things that answer back is a training in honesty that I do not think can be had any other way. People who work only with words and numbers can go a long time without being told clearly that they are wrong. Their errors are absorbed by ambiguity. Someone who makes objects is corrected constantly and without malice, and learns to prefer being corrected. I have noticed that the people I most respect in any field are the ones who stay close to some part of reality that can contradict them.

It also keeps a person modest about cleverness. I enjoy ideas as much as anyone, and I work in fields full of them. But an idea is a claim about the world, and the world has the last word. The longer I work, the more I want my ideas to pass through something that can refuse them: a prototype, a price someone actually pays, a part that has to fit.

So when I say tangible, I mean answerable. I want to be judged by things that do not care what I say about them. They are harder to satisfy than any audience, and what they approve tends to last.
