Every number I read is one of three things, and I try to know which before I use it.

A fact is something that was measured or reported: the revenue a company published for last quarter, the number of shares a fund declared in a filing. An estimate is a number someone worked out from partial information, such as a company's share of its market, where nobody has the full count. A model is the output of assumptions run forward, like the size a market will reach in five years.

The three deserve very different amounts of trust, and most analysis presents them in exactly the same way. A sentence such as "the company has 30% of a market that will be worth 400 billion by 2030" contains an estimate and a model, written with the confidence of a fact. The reader cannot tell which part was observed and which part was assumed, so they either believe all of it or none of it. Both are mistakes.

The problem gets worse when numbers are combined. Multiply a fact by an estimate and then by a model, and the result has the reliability of its weakest part, while looking as precise as its strongest. A valuation built this way can be quoted to the dollar and still be little more than an opinion.

So in THE ENTITY I made a rule that every figure carries a label saying what it is: fact, estimate or model. It also carries a date and a grade for how confident we are in it. This costs almost nothing to do, a word next to a number, and it changes how the number can be used. Someone can decide to act only on facts, or to accept estimates but treat every model as a scenario.

I apply the same habit to my own decisions. When I plan a product or a release, most of what I write down is a model: how many people will want it, what it will cost to make, how long it will take. Calling it a model, in writing, stops me from defending it later as if I had known. When reality comes in, I replace the model with the fact and look at the distance between the two. That distance, measured often enough, is the most useful thing I know about my own judgement.

There is also a simple test for other people's work. Take any confident paragraph and ask of each number which of the three it is. If the author cannot say, the paragraph was written to persuade, and I read the rest of it accordingly.
