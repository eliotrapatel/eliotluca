When I worked around professional investors, the thing that struck me was not that they were smarter than private investors. Many were, some were not. What they all had was different material in front of them.

An individual who follows a sector reads the news, a few company reports and what other people post. A professional desk has a data terminal that costs around two thousand dollars a month, a service that turns regulatory filings into something readable, analysts who know which supplier feeds which customer, and colleagues whose job is to argue with every thesis before any money moves. By the time a story reaches the news, that desk has usually seen its ingredients for weeks.

For a long time I took this gap as a fact of life. It looked like a matter of access: some information was simply reserved for people who could pay for it. When I looked closer, that turned out to be mostly wrong. The filings are public. Prices are public. Company announcements are public. What is not public is the work of connecting them: knowing that a memory shortage in one country matters to a server maker in another, and to the three companies that depend on that server maker. The edge is in the structure, and structure is something software can hold.

That is the idea behind THE ENTITY. I did not try to find secret information. I took what is already available and did the connecting work once, properly, for a whole industry: every company placed on the part of the AI supply chain it supplies or depends on, every dependency drawn as a link. An individual investor can then ask the same question a desk asks, which is who is exposed to this, and get an answer in seconds.

Two things made this possible now and not ten years ago. The cost of processing large amounts of text has fallen sharply, so reading every filing of several hundred companies is no longer a job for a room of analysts. And interfaces have improved enough that a complicated model can be shown to someone who has never used a terminal.

I think this pattern goes well beyond investing. In many fields the professionals are ahead because they have tools that organise public information, and those tools were built for a few thousand customers at a very high price. Each one of them can be rebuilt for a hundred times more people at a hundredth of the cost. I expect a good share of the useful companies of the next few years to do exactly that, and I intend to keep building in that direction.
