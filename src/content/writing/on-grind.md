One of the lines I wrote for ORBES says that the brand celebrates "a journey of grind, offering a fresh perspective on self-realization." The word grind has a poor reputation, and part of it is deserved. It has been used to glorify exhaustion, to make a virtue of sleeping little and to sell people the idea that suffering is a strategy. I want to say what I mean by it, because I mean something narrower.

Grind, as I use the word, is the long repetition of unglamorous work in the service of something I chose. The definition has three parts and each one matters. Long, because nothing worth having is built in a season. Unglamorous, because most of the work behind any result is invisible and dull: the tenth revision, the thousandth line, the spreadsheet nobody will ever see. Chosen, because effort imposed by someone else, toward an end I do not care about, is only labour.

What separates grind from mere busyness is direction. A busy person does a great deal and ends the year where they started. I can usually tell which of the two I am doing by asking what the day's work added to. If it added to something that will still exist in five years, it was grind. If it only kept things moving, it was motion.

Why would this lead to self-realisation? Because I do not think we find out who we are by reflection. We find out by what we are able to sustain. Anyone can be enthusiastic about a field for a month. The fields I am still working in after years, on the days when nothing about them is exciting, are the ones that turned out to be mine. Effort is a filter. It removes the things I only liked the idea of.

It is also how a skill stops being something I do and becomes part of what I am. After enough repetition I no longer think about the fundamentals of a discipline. They have moved into my hands and my eye, and what is left free is judgement. I know of no shortcut to that state, and I have stopped looking for one.

Camus ended his essay on Sisyphus by saying that we must imagine him happy, pushing his stone up the hill for ever. I have always liked the sentence and disagreed with the situation. Sisyphus did not choose his stone, and it goes nowhere. I chose mine, and each day it is a little higher than the day before. I do not need to imagine being happy about that.

None of this is an argument against rest. Effort without recovery is vanity, and it produces worse work. But rest, for me, is in service of the work, and I would not want it the other way round.
