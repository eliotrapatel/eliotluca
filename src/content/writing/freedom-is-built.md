Freedom is usually pictured as an absence: no boss, no obligations, nobody telling you what to do. I used to think of it that way. I now think that picture describes a holiday, and that real freedom is something a person constructs.

The philosopher Isaiah Berlin distinguished two meanings of the word. One is freedom from: not being interfered with. The other is the freedom to be one's own master, to direct one's life oneself. The first is what most people have in mind, and it matters. But the second is the one that decides how a life goes, and I would push it one step further: to be your own master, you have to be able to do things. A person whom nobody restrains, and who has no skills, no savings and no command of himself, is free in a sense that is of very little use to him.

So I think of freedom as a capacity, and I see three materials it is made from.

The first is capital. Money is often discussed as a way to buy things, and that is its least interesting use. What capital really holds is options. It lets me refuse an offer that does not suit me, wait for a better moment, survive a mistake, or spend a year on something that will not pay yet. Someone with no reserves has to accept the first terms they are given. I manage my capital carefully for this reason more than for any other.

The second is skill. Every discipline I acquire is a door that was closed before. Being able to design means I do not wait for a designer. Being able to read a balance sheet means I cannot easily be misled about one. Each skill removes a dependence, and dependence is the real opposite of freedom. The more I can do myself, the fewer people hold a key to my plans.

The third is self-command, and without it the other two are wasted. A person who cannot govern his own attention, his impulses or his moods is ruled by whatever stimulates him, however rich and capable he is. I have met people with every external freedom who were obviously not free, because they could not sit down and do what they had decided to do.

This leads to something that looks like a paradox and is not one. Freedom requires discipline. The hours I spend learning, saving and training myself are constrained hours, and they are exactly what enlarge what I am able to do later. The person who refuses all constraint in the name of freedom ends up with very little of it.

I do not expect to finish this construction. Freedom built this way is never complete, and it can be lost through carelessness. But each year I can do more than the year before, on my own terms, and that is the most reliable measure of freedom I know.
