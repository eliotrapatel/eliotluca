Epictetus opens his handbook with a division. Some things are up to us: our judgements, our intentions, what we choose to pursue. Other things are not: our body, our reputation, what other people do, how events turn out. He says that most unhappiness comes from confusing the two.

I wrote my own version of this before I had read him, in a page I called *Vision*: nothing outside me should be able to move my mind, my emotions or my motivation. When I later found the same idea in a text almost two thousand years old, I was less surprised than reassured. It meant I had reached something true enough to be rediscovered.

But I do not draw the Stoic conclusion from it. The Stoics go on to say that since outcomes are not up to us, we should hold them lightly, almost with indifference. I cannot do that, and I do not want to. I care a great deal about outcomes. I want my companies to succeed and my work to last. So I use the division for something else.

First, it tells me where to put effort. Everything I have goes into the part that is mine: what I learn, what I decide, how well I execute, how I behave when it goes badly. I do not spend energy resenting a market or wishing a person were different. This is less noble than it sounds. It is simply that effort spent on what I cannot change returns nothing.

Second, and here I part from the ancients, I do not take the border between the two as fixed. A great deal of what seems outside my control is only outside it today. I cannot control a client, but I can make sure that no single client matters too much. I cannot control a price, but I can decide how exposed I am to it. Much of my work consists of moving things from the second list to the first, or at least shrinking what the second list is able to reach.

The same applies to emotion. I do not try to feel less. An emotion is information, usually accurate, about my own state: that I am tired, or afraid of losing something, or attached to a plan. It is rarely accurate about the world. So I let it arrive, I note what it says about me, and I leave some time between the feeling and any decision. That delay is the most practical freedom I know of. It is small and entirely mine.

What remains truly outside my reach, I try to meet without complaint. I think that is the part of Stoicism worth keeping. The rest I would rather change than accept.
