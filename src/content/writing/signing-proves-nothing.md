I have seen an acquisition from two places. The first was before a deal, helping to prepare the information memorandum and the answers to buyers' questions for an €80M sale. The second was after one, working on the integration of an acquired company inside a larger group. They are the same story at two moments, and what struck me is how little the people at the first moment think about the second.

Before a deal, all the effort goes into an argument: here is the business, and here is why it is worth this price. The memorandum makes the argument and the buyers test it. They ask which customers could leave, which employees matter most, which contracts end if the owner changes. It is serious work, and by the end both sides share a detailed picture of what the business will be worth once it is combined with the buyer's.

That picture is a forecast. On the day of signing, none of the extra value in it exists yet. The savings from merging two teams, the sales from offering one company's product to the other's customers, the cost avoided by sharing one system: all of it still has to be done, by people who were mostly not in the room and have not read the documents.

What I saw during integration is that this value is rarely lost through a bad decision. It is lost because nobody in particular was asked to deliver it. On the day the deal is approved, a saving belongs to the whole deal team. Six months later the deal team has moved on, finance is reporting a number, the operating teams are busy with their normal work, and the saving sits somewhere between them.

So the first thing I would insist on in any deal sounds dull. Each expected gain gets one named person, a date and a figure anyone can check. "Procurement savings" is a hope. "These two software contracts merged into one by the end of the second quarter, owned by this person" is a task.

The second is to decide who does what before the announcement, even roughly. Who reports to whom, which team keeps which function, which name customers will see. If those questions are still open on the day people hear the news, they answer them for themselves, usually pessimistically, and the people with the most options start listening to other offers. A provisional answer that changes later does far less damage than silence.

The third is to find the people who hold the knowledge that is written nowhere. In most companies a few people know why a certain client is billed a certain way, which system fails at the end of each month, and what was promised to whom. They are often not senior, and they are not on any retention list. If they leave during the transition, part of what was bought leaves with them.

I build my own companies now, and I see the same pattern far from M&A. A fundraising deck is a memorandum, and a product launch is a signing. Both are forecasts. What makes them come true is the work that follows, and that work only gets done when each promise has been given to one person with a date.
