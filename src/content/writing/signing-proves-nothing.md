Everyone celebrates the signing. The people who will run the combined business should be the least excited people in the room.

I have worked on both sides of that moment. On one side, I helped prepare the information memorandum and the Q&A for an €80M M&A deal: the document that explains why a business is worth buying, and the answers that defend that explanation line by line. On the other, in corporate development at Amundi Technology, I worked on post-merger integration, the part where two organisations described as complementary have to operate as one from a given Monday morning.

The two jobs taught me the same lesson from opposite ends. Signing is the moment the work starts, and at that moment most of the value in the deal is still unproven.

## The memorandum tells a story

An information memorandum is a sales document with discipline. It sets out the market, the business, the numbers and the case for the price. Good ones are honest. All of them are selective, because their purpose is to make a reader see a future that does not exist yet.

The Q&A that follows is where buyers push on that story. They ask about customer concentration, about key people, about which contracts carry change-of-control clauses. Preparing those answers teaches you which parts of the story are solid and which parts rest on an assumption.

Integration tests every assumption at once, and it does so through people who never read the memorandum. The customer deciding whether to renew has no interest in the equity story. The engineer deciding whether to stay has no interest in the multiple. The story survives contact with them, or it does not.

## Synergies are hypotheses

Every deal model has a synergy line: cost savings from merged functions, revenue from cross-selling, efficiency from shared platforms. On paper they look like numbers. In practice they are hypotheses with a deadline, and a hypothesis with no owner is a wish.

Synergies tend to belong to everyone at the investment committee and to no one six months later. Finance tracks them, the business units are expected to deliver them, and value leaks out through the gap between those two sentences. Nobody decides to lose it. It goes because the work of capturing it was never assigned to a person with a calendar.

## What I would insist on

**Decide the operating model before the announcement.** Who reports to whom, which entity keeps which function, which brand faces the customer. If these questions are still open on announcement day, the organisation fills the silence with rumour, and the best people start taking calls from recruiters. A provisional answer that can be adjusted later beats an open question.

**Name one owner per synergy, with a date and a metric.** One person, never a committee. A date by which the effect should appear. A metric anyone can read without opening the model. "Procurement savings" is a category. Consolidated licence contracts, owned by a named person, visible in the cost base by a stated quarter, is a commitment.

**Protect the people who carry the knowledge.** In many acquisitions the most valuable asset is undocumented: knowing why a client is billed the way it is, which interface breaks at month-end, which promise was made to which customer. The people who hold that knowledge are often neither senior nor on the retention list. Find them early, tell them what their role will be, and give them a reason to stay through the transition.

**Integrate systems in the order customers feel them.** The temptation is to start with what is convenient internally, the general ledger or the intranet. Customers see none of that. They see invoices, support, logins, and the contact who picks up the phone. Sequence the work so the customer-facing surface is stable first, then fix the back office behind it.

**Communicate more than feels necessary.** Inside the deal team the plan feels obvious, because everyone has lived with it for months. Everyone else is hearing it for the first time. Repeat the operating model, the timeline and what changes for each team, in writing, more often than seems reasonable. Silence is read as bad news, and people act on bad news faster than on good.

**Measure what you promised the investment committee.** The committee approved a price on the strength of specific claims. Track those claims after closing, in the same terms, with the original hypotheses and their actual values side by side, rather than a new dashboard built to look good. It is the only honest way to learn whether the deal worked, and the only way to price the next one better.

## The same rule outside M&A

I now build companies rather than prepare them for sale, and the lesson travels. A pitch deck is a short information memorandum: a story about a future. A launch is a signing. Neither proves anything on the day. What proves it is the plain work afterwards: an owner for each promise, a date, a metric, and the people who know how things actually run.

Signing gives you the right to try. The integration plan should exist before the signature does.
