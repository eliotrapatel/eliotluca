In 2026 I started learning watchmaking. It is the newest of my disciplines and the one that surprises people most, so I want to explain what I am looking for in it.

A mechanical watch is a machine with nothing hidden. Energy is stored in a coiled spring. A train of wheels carries it to the escapement, which releases it in small equal steps. A balance wheel, swinging back and forth several times a second, decides how long each step lasts. That is the entire principle. There is no software, no battery and no part whose behaviour you cannot see with a loupe.

After years of working mostly in software, this is what attracted me. Software is forgiving. If something is wrong, you correct it and release again, and the user may never know. A watch does not allow that. A part is the right size or it is not, and a movement that has been badly assembled will show it within a day. I wanted to train in a field where the result is physical and final, because I think that habit of mind is fading among people who only build on screens.

The second attraction is what a watch teaches about error. A good mechanical watch may gain four seconds a day. That is an accuracy of about 46 parts in a million, which is remarkable for a device made of springs and wheels. And yet those four seconds, left alone, become more than 24 minutes over a year. I built the [Beat](/instruments/beat) instrument to make that visible. The lesson applies far beyond watches. A small constant error is harmless on any given day and decisive over a long period, in a budget, in a habit or in a company. Watchmakers do not eliminate error. They measure it and regulate it, again and again, and I find that a more realistic model of improvement than most advice about productivity.

The third reason is ORBES. I make objects for men to wear and keep for years, and I do not think I can do that well without understanding how fine mechanical things are made: how parts are finished, how they fit, what tolerance a hand can feel. Watchmaking is the most demanding school for that which I know of.

I am at the beginning, and I am in no hurry. Part of what I like is that this field cannot be rushed. It rewards patience and steady hands, two things my other work rarely asks of me, and I would like to have more of both.
